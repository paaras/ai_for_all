Two ways to make a computer smart

Way 1: people write all the rules by hand. For example: "If an email says FREE, it is spam."

Way 2: the computer figures out the rules by looking at lots of examples. This is what neural networks do.

Hand-written rules work for simple cases, but the real world keeps finding ways around them.

The idea came from the brain

Your brain is made of cells called neurons.

A neuron receives small signals from other neurons. If enough signals arrive at about the same time, it fires and sends a signal of its own to the next neurons.

Your brain has about 86 billion neurons, and each one connects to thousands of others.

Try it: send signals into the neuron. One at a time fades away. Several close together make it fire.

 

A bird and a plane both fly

People studied birds to learn how to fly, but planes do not flap their wings. They borrowed the idea and built something different.

Neural networks are the same. They borrowed the idea of neurons from the brain, but they work very differently. Each has strengths the other does not.

An artificial neuron

An artificial neuron is a tiny calculation.

  1. It takes in a few pieces of information. Here, each is 1 (yes) or 0 (no).
  2. Each piece has a weight that says how much it matters. A negative weight pushes toward "no".
  3. It multiplies each piece by its weight and adds everything up.
  4. If the total reaches 3, the answer is yes.

Try it: tap the inputs and move the weights. Thicker lines matter more. Red lines push toward "no".

The weights are what it learns

The weights are the "adjustable numbers" from last week. Learning just means finding weights that give the right answers.

One neuron can only draw a straight line

When a single neuron sorts things into two groups, the dividing line it uses is always straight.

That works when the groups sit on opposite sides. It fails when one group surrounds the other.

Connect neurons together

A neural network is many neurons connected in layers.

  • The first layer takes in the information.
  • The middle layers, called hidden layers, pass it along and combine it.
  • The last layer gives the answer.

Each neuron's answer becomes an input for every neuron in the next layer. Every connection has its own weight.

Try it: send a signal through. Tap any neuron to see its connections.

More neurons, more complicated patterns

Each neuron draws one straight line. Combine many of them across layers and their lines bend together into curves and loops.

Nobody tells the network where the lines go. It finds them by practicing on the dots.

How a network learns

Nobody sets the weights by hand. The network learns them in a loop:

  1. Guess. Look at one example and give an answer.
  2. Check. Compare the guess to the right answer.
  3. Nudge. Change every weight a tiny bit in the direction that would have made the guess better.

Repeat this millions of times and the weights settle into good values. Here the neuron learns the six days from two slides ago on its own.

Try it: press "One round" a few times and watch the weights move. Then press "100 rounds".

Layers learn building blocks

In networks that look at pictures, the layers tend to split up the work:

  • Early layers notice simple things like edges and lines.
  • Middle layers combine those into shapes like loops and corners.
  • Later layers recognize the whole thing.

This small network learned to read handwritten numbers from about 1,800 examples. It sees your drawing shrunk down to an 8 by 8 grid.

Nobody knows what every connection means

After training, a network is just a huge pile of weights. We can see what goes in and what comes out, but the reasons in the middle are hard to read. That is why neural networks are called black boxes.

This matters when a network makes an important decision, like approving a loan, and nobody can explain why.

Researchers are working on this. In large models they have found some neurons that respond to specific ideas, but most connections are still a mystery.

Try it: these are the 32 hidden neurons from the number reader you just used. Tap one to see the 64 weights coming into it.

Memorizing is not learning

A network can get every practice example right just by memorizing them, and then fail on anything new. This is called overfitting.

It is like a student who memorized last year's test answers instead of learning the material.

To catch this, some examples are kept aside and never used for practice. They work like a final exam with new questions.

Bad examples make a bad network

A network only knows what it was shown. If one group of people is rare in the examples, the network learns the pattern for everyone else and gets that group wrong more often.

This has happened for real. Some face recognition systems made far more mistakes on darker-skinned women, because their training photos were mostly of lighter-skinned men.

Try it: Group A and Group B follow slightly different patterns, and the network can't tell which group a dot belongs to. Change how much of the training data comes from Group B and compare how often it is right for each group.

With 3%, the network simply learns Group A's pattern. With 50%, the mistakes are shared more fairly. Doing well for both groups needs plenty of examples from both.

Where neural networks are used today

Medical scans

Spot signs of disease in X-rays, eye scans, and skin photos, often as a second opinion for a doctor.

Speech to text

Turn your voice into words for captions, dictation, and voice assistants.

Translation

Translate text and speech between more than a hundred languages.

Self-driving cars

Recognize lanes, signs, cars, and people from camera images in real time.

Weather forecasts

Learn from decades of past weather to predict the next days quickly.

New medicines

Predict the 3D shape of proteins, which helps scientists design new drugs.

Your phone

Face unlock, photo search, and keyboard suggestions all run small neural networks.

Chat AI

ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are giant neural networks that predict the next word.

Code is exact instructions

Code is a list of instructions for a computer. The computer follows them exactly, every time. It does what you said, not what you meant.

That is why code can be frustrating to write: one wrong step and the result is wrong.

You do not need to write code yourself. The AI writes it for you. Your job is to describe clearly what you want, and to check that the result works.

What makes a web page

Every web page and web app has three parts:

  • Content: the words, buttons, and pictures.
  • Look: the colors, sizes, and layout.
  • Actions: what happens when you tap or type.

Think of a house. The content is the walls and rooms, the look is the paint and furniture, and the actions are the electricity that makes things work.

Each part has its own kind of code, but you only need to know they exist. The AI writes all three.

Try it: turn each part off and see what is missing.
