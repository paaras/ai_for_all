How a regular model answers

A language model writes one token, or piece of a word, at a time, and each token gets one pass through the model.

If the model writes the answer first, it only gets one pass to figure it out, so on a tricky question it gives the quick answer like most people do.

Chain of thought

If you ask a model to think step by step, it writes out its work before the answer and gets more questions right.

This is called chain of thought. It works for the same reason scratch paper helps you with math.

Why writing the steps helps

Every word the model writes becomes part of what it reads next, so it can use the steps it already wrote.

Attention is the part of the model that decides which earlier words to focus on. It lets the model look back at the steps it needs, like you would look back at your own work.

What happens when you turn on thinking

A reasoning model has been trained to do chain of thought on its own. When you turn thinking on, it writes its thinking first and then the answer. Most apps hide the thinking or show a short summary of it.

The effort setting controls how much it can think. More effort usually gives better answers to hard questions, but it's slower and uses more of your usage limit.

Most models now decide when to think

The first reasoning models, like OpenAI's o1 in 2024, always thought before answering. Now most models can answer either way and decide on their own how much to think.

Apps call this different things, like Thinking, Think or Extended thinking, but it's the same idea.

What the thinking looks like

The thinking on a hard problem looks like someone working it out on paper. The model makes a plan, tries something, checks it, and goes back if it doesn't work.

Nobody programmed these steps. The model learned them in training because they lead to more right answers.

    How a model learns to think

    After learning from a huge amount of text, the model practices on problems with answers that can be checked, like math, puzzles and code.

    It tries each problem many times and only the final answer is graded. Its weights, the numbers it learned in training, are then adjusted toward the thinking that got it right. This is called reinforcement learning.

    More thinking, better answers

    OpenAI tested o1, its first reasoning model, on a hard high school math contest. GPT-4o, their regular model, solved 12% of the problems and o1 solved 74%.

    It did even better when it solved each problem many times and picked its most common answer. Wrong answers are usually different each time, but the right answer keeps coming back.

    The strongest modes today, like Gemini Deep Think and ChatGPT Pro, do something similar by thinking through several ideas at once and comparing them.

    Try it: press Add more thinking

    Thinking with tools

    Newer models can use tools while they think. They can search the web, run code to do math, or zoom in on a photo, then keep thinking about what they found.

    This lets the model look things up and check its math instead of guessing.

    Longer and longer tasks

    Because models can now think, use tools and check their work, they can work on a task for a long time without help. AI that does this is often called an agent.

    A research group called METR measures how long a task AI can finish on its own about half the time, based on how long it takes a person. The length doubles every few months, from a few minutes in 2024 to more than a full workday in 2026.

    Thinking can still be wrong

    The thinking is made of likely tokens like any other text, so a step can be wrong, and every step after it builds on the mistake.

    Thinking also can't fix facts the model never learned, so still check anything important.

    Try it: one step is wrong, tap it

    Dinner was $84. We're leaving a 20% tip and splitting it between 4 people. How much does each person pay?

    The thinking isn't always the real reason

    Researchers added hints to multiple choice questions, like a note saying a professor picked A. The models often switched to the hinted answer but usually didn't mention the hint in their thinking.

    So the thinking is useful for spotting mistakes, but it doesn't always show why the model chose its answer.

    Try it: compare the question with and without the hint

    Models come in different sizes

    Most companies make their models in a few sizes. Claude has Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, and Gemini has Flash-Lite, Flash and Pro, from smallest to largest.

    Bigger models have more weights, so they know more and handle harder tasks, but they're slower and cost more. Smaller models are fast and cheap and work fine for most everyday questions.

    Try it: tap a size

    A rough comparison

    Size and thinking are two different choices

    Size and thinking are separate choices. In Gemini, Flash and Thinking are the same model with thinking off and on, and Pro is a bigger model that also thinks. Claude's Sonnet works the same way, a medium model you can use with thinking on or off.

    Size decides how much the model knows, and thinking decides how much work it does before answering. A small model that thinks can beat a big model that doesn't on a math problem, but thinking won't help with facts it never learned.

    Try it: tap each box

    Mixture of experts

    Normally every token goes through every weight in the model, which makes big models slow and expensive.

    A mixture of experts model is split into many smaller parts called experts. A router sends each token to only a few of them, so a huge model only uses a small part of itself for each token.

    Small models can learn from big ones

    A big reasoning model can solve lots of problems and write out its thinking. A small model can then be trained on those examples. This is called distillation.

    The small model learns to work through problems step by step too, which is a big reason small models have gotten so much better.
